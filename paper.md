# TargetTrace: A Dual-Task Drug-Target Interaction Model Combining Multi-Radius Fingerprints, Stacked Cross-Attention over ESM-2 Residues, and Molecule-Level Negative Training

---

## Abstract

Accurate prediction of drug-target interactions (DTIs) is a central challenge in computational drug discovery. We present TargetTrace, a deep learning model that jointly predicts binary binding probability and potency (pIC50) from molecular fingerprints and protein sequence representations. The model employs an 8,379-dimensional multi-radius fingerprint encoding, a two-layer cross-attention mechanism that allows the molecular embedding to directly query per-residue ESM-2 hidden states, and a Hadamard interaction fusion layer. Critically, we demonstrate that including experimentally measured inactive compounds as explicit negative training examples — alongside the conventional protein-shuffle protocol — is both necessary and sufficient to achieve strong molecule-level discrimination. On an external PDBbind benchmark curated from the RCSB Protein Data Bank, the full model achieves AUC-ROC = 0.854 (95% CI: 0.823–0.882), AUPRC = 0.969 (0.957–0.978), and BEDROC = 0.994 (0.984–1.000), with Spearman ρ = 0.628 (0.579–0.674) for pIC50 prediction. A systematic ablation study reveals that architectural improvements alone are insufficient: stacked cross-attention without real inactive examples raises internal validation AUC from 0.680 to 0.795 but collapses external AUC to 0.487, while the addition of molecule-level negatives restores and substantially improves performance (0.487 → 0.854). Chemical similarity analysis confirms that 24.7% of PDBbind test compounds lie below a Tanimoto threshold of 0.4 to any training compound, with AUC remaining well above chance (0.766) even for this most-novel quartile, demonstrating genuine structural generalisation. The model is overconfident (expected calibration error = 0.178), and temperature scaling is recommended before using raw sigmoid outputs as calibrated probabilities.

## 1. Introduction

The identification of small molecules that selectively bind to therapeutically relevant protein targets — broadly termed drug-target interaction (DTI) prediction — constitutes a foundational step in early-stage drug discovery. Experimental assay campaigns are expensive, time-consuming, and capable of covering only a negligible fraction of the vast chemical space of drug-like molecules, estimated to comprise between 10²³ and 10⁶⁰ compounds. Computational DTI models address this bottleneck by prioritising molecules for synthesis and experimental testing, and their accuracy and calibration are therefore of considerable practical importance.

Early machine learning approaches to DTI prediction relied on sparse binary fingerprints to encode molecules and on simple sequence-derived features or one-hot encodings to represent proteins, typically combined with support vector machines or shallow neural networks. The advent of deep learning enabled qualitatively richer molecular and protein representations. Graph neural networks (GNNs) learn directly from molecular graphs, capturing topological features that fingerprints cannot fully represent. Recurrent and transformer architectures exploit long-range sequence dependencies in proteins, while pre-trained protein language models (PLMs) such as ESM-2 provide transferable, residue-level representations trained on hundreds of millions of natural sequences. Recent DTI models including DeepDTA, AttentionDTA, MolBERT, and ECIF have made considerable progress by combining such representations with interaction-modelling components. Despite these advances, two challenges remain incompletely addressed: the quality of negative training examples and the granularity of protein representation at the residue level.

The construction of negative examples for DTI training is a subtle but consequential design decision. Most large-scale bioactivity databases contain predominantly confirmed binding pairs; negative evidence — compounds that have been explicitly tested and found inactive — is far less systematically curated. In practice, the dominant strategy is protein-shuffle: molecules from the training set are randomly paired with proteins they are not known to bind, creating pseudo-negative examples at negligible cost. While protein-shuffle negatives are easy to generate at scale, they create an artifactual training objective: the model must learn to distinguish correct molecule–protein associations from random, biologically implausible pairings. This task is easily solved by learning a low-complexity protein-identity classifier, and it does not prepare the model for the operationally relevant challenge of virtual screening, in which the protein target is fixed and the model must rank a library of diverse, drug-like molecules by predicted affinity. The resulting misalignment between training objective and deployment objective can produce models that achieve high internal validation performance while failing substantially in prospective screening experiments.

A second challenge concerns how the protein is represented in the model. Many DTI architectures encode proteins as fixed-length pooled vectors, collapsing per-residue information into a single summary vector before any interaction with the molecular representation. This discards positional information that is directly relevant to ligand binding: different regions of the protein surface contribute very differently to binding energetics, and the ligand's interaction with each residue position depends on its own structural features. Cross-attention mechanisms offer a principled remedy, allowing the molecular query to attend selectively over residue positions and thereby construct a context-dependent protein representation that reflects the particular ligand under consideration.

In this work we present TargetTrace, a DTI model that addresses both challenges simultaneously. We make five distinct contributions. We construct an 8,379-dimensional multi-radius molecular fingerprint by concatenating ECFP at radii 1, 2, and 3, FCFP4 pharmacophoric fingerprints, MACCS keys, and 200 RDKit 2D descriptors, capturing local, medium-range, and distal chemical environments within a single compact representation. We introduce a two-layer stacked cross-attention architecture in which the molecular embedding iteratively queries the ESM-2 per-residue tensor, with a residual connection ensuring that first-layer signals are preserved. We employ a Hadamard interaction fusion layer that explicitly computes element-wise products of molecular and protein embeddings, providing rich pairwise features without the full quadratic cost of bilinear interaction. Critically, we incorporate 423,825 experimentally measured inactive compound–target pairs (Ki/IC50/Kd > 10 µM from ChEMBL) as explicit negative training examples alongside protein-shuffle negatives, providing a training signal that directly targets molecule-activity discrimination. Finally, the model optimises both binary binding classification and pIC50 regression simultaneously through a combined BCE and Huber loss, sharing all intermediate representations between the two tasks.

Through a systematic ablation study across four model configurations, supported by bootstrap statistical testing, calibration assessment, per-target performance breakdown, and chemical similarity characterisation, we demonstrate that molecule-level negative training is the decisive architectural choice enabling strong external performance. Adding measured inactives raises PDBbind AUC from 0.487 to 0.854, and the resulting model shows meaningful generalisation to chemically novel scaffolds while maintaining interpretable cross-attention behaviour over functionally relevant protein regions.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Dataset Curation

All bioactivity data were retrieved from ChEMBL (release 33) using a bootstrapped extraction pipeline targeting human protein targets with at least one valid activity measurement. Records were retained if they possessed a valid canonical SMILES string, a full protein sequence, and a numerical activity measurement of type Ki, Kd, IC50, or EC50 expressed in nanomolar units. After applying these filters, a total of 3,035,729 compound–target pairs spanning 425 unique human protein targets and 222,371 unique compounds were collected. A compound was labelled active (y = 1) if its measured activity was at or below 10,000 nM (10 µM), consistent with standard ChEMBL hit criteria, yielding 2,611,904 active pairs and 423,825 inactive pairs. The continuous potency value was transformed as pIC50 = 9 − log₁₀(activity/nM) for all records; across active pairs the mean pIC50 was 6.58 with a standard deviation of 1.38.

Molecular representations were computed with RDKit. The final fingerprint vector concatenates Morgan circular fingerprints at radii 1, 2, and 3 (ECFP2, ECFP4, ECFP6; 2,048 bits each), FCFP4 pharmacophoric fingerprints (2,048 bits), MACCS structural keys (167 bits), and 200 RDKit 2D physicochemical descriptors after standard-score normalisation, yielding an 8,379-dimensional sparse-dense hybrid input vector per compound. Protein sequences were embedded using ESM-2 (esm2_t33_650M_UR50D), a 650M-parameter protein language model pre-trained on UniRef50. The model was applied in frozen inference mode to produce both per-residue hidden states of dimension 480 and mean-pooled sequence-level vectors. Per-residue tensors were cached to disk in compressed format and loaded in full onto GPU as a (N_proteins × L_max × 480) tensor at the start of each training session, enabling O(1) lookup by protein index without repeated ESM-2 forward passes.

### 2.2 Model Architecture

TargetTrace is a dual-task encoder–fusion network comprising 10,604,674 trainable parameters. The molecule branch processes the 8,379-dimensional fingerprint through a three-layer MLP to produce a 256-dimensional molecular embedding. The first linear layer maps from 8,379 to 1,024 dimensions, followed by SiLU activation, batch normalisation, and dropout (p = 0.4). A second linear layer reduces to 512 dimensions with the same normalisation sequence and dropout (p = 0.3), and a final linear layer maps to the 256-dimensional output with SiLU activation applied thereafter.

The protein branch uses a two-layer stacked cross-attention module to construct a protein context vector conditioned on the molecular query. In the first cross-attention layer, the 256-dimensional molecular embedding **m** serves as the query, while the ESM-2 per-residue tensor **R** ∈ ℝ^(L×480) provides keys and values. Multi-head attention with 4 heads and a key dimension of 64 produces a first-layer protein context vector **p**₁ ∈ ℝ^256. Padding positions are masked to −∞ before the softmax operation, and layer normalisation is applied after the output projection. In the second cross-attention layer, **p**₁ serves as the new query, attending over the same residue tensor **R** to produce a refined representation **p**₂ ∈ ℝ^256. A residual connection yields the final protein embedding **p** = **p**₂ + **p**₁, ensuring that first-layer signals are preserved even when the second layer learns only an incremental correction.

The molecular and protein embeddings are combined through a Hadamard interaction and fusion stage. The concatenation [**m** ‖ **p** ‖ **m** ⊙ **p**] ∈ ℝ^768, where ⊙ denotes element-wise multiplication, is passed through a fusion MLP that maps from 768 to 256 dimensions (SiLU, batch normalisation, dropout p = 0.3) and then to 128 dimensions (SiLU), yielding the joint representation **h** ∈ ℝ^128. Two independent linear output heads map **h** to a scalar binding logit and a normalised pIC50 estimate, respectively. Binding probability is obtained by applying the sigmoid function to the logit; pIC50 predictions are denormalised using training-set statistics at evaluation time.

### 2.3 Training Procedure

A Murcko scaffold split partitioned the 222,371 unique compounds into training (80%) and validation (20%) sets, ensuring that no Murcko scaffold appeared in both partitions. This procedure prevents molecule-level data leakage while retaining the full set of training protein targets in both splits. The resulting training set comprised 2,086,993 positive pairs and 341,575 inactive pairs; the validation set comprised 607,161 pairs. pIC50 values were normalised using the mean and standard deviation computed from training-set active pairs and denormalised after inference.

Each training step combined two sources of negative signal. Protein-shuffle negatives were generated at the batch level: for each positive (molecule, protein) pair in a batch of B = 512 samples, a protein index was sampled uniformly from the batch without replacement, constrained to differ from the true protein index. These negatives require the model to assign higher binding probability to correct molecule–protein associations than to randomly permuted pairings, providing a readily available and scalable source of gradient signal. In parallel, a concurrent data loader sampled batches of 256 measured inactive pairs from the 341,575 ChEMBL inactive training records, cycling through the full inactive dataset with itertools.cycle to ensure complete coverage across epochs. These real molecule-level negatives couple an experimentally confirmed inactive compound with its verified non-binding target, providing a training signal that requires the model to distinguish active from inactive molecules against the same protein — the precise task evaluated in virtual screening.

The total loss per batch is the sum of binary cross-entropy computed over all three pair types (positives, protein-shuffle negatives, and real molecule-level negatives) and a Huber regression loss (δ = 1.0) weighted by 0.3 and computed only over samples with valid pIC50 labels:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}\!\left(\hat{y}_{\text{act}}, y_{\text{act}}\right) + 0.3 \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{Huber}}\!\left(\hat{y}_{\text{pIC50}}, y_{\text{pIC50}}\right)$$

The model was optimised with AdamW (learning rate = 1×10⁻⁴, weight decay = 1×10⁻³) using cosine annealing with T_max = 20 epochs and η_min = 1×10⁻⁶. Mixed-precision training (PyTorch AMP) was employed throughout, with gradient norms clipped to 1.0. Early stopping with patience of 5 epochs was applied based on the protein-shuffle validation AUC-ROC, and the checkpoint with the highest validation AUC was retained. Training converged at epoch 18, requiring approximately 98 minutes on a single NVIDIA GPU.

### 2.4 Internal Evaluation Protocol

After each training epoch, the model was evaluated on the scaffold-split validation set under the protein-shuffle protocol. For each validation batch, a shuffled protein index is paired with each molecule, and AUC-ROC is computed over true positives and shuffle negatives. This measures the model's ability to assign higher binding probability to correct molecule–protein associations than to random pairings, and it served as the criterion for early stopping and checkpoint selection. Regression performance on pIC50 was characterised by mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), Spearman rank correlation (ρ), and Pearson correlation (r) computed over all positive pairs with valid pIC50 annotations.

### 2.5 External Evaluation Dataset

To assess real-world virtual screening performance on independently measured data, we curated an evaluation set from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. The 425 UniProt accession codes corresponding to TargetTrace training targets were queried against the RCSB Search API with a filter requiring annotated binding affinity data sourced from BindingDB, returning 1,555 candidate PDB entries. A GraphQL batch query to the RCSB Data API retrieved, for each entry, the polymer entity UniProt accession identifiers, non-polymer ligand chemical component IDs, and rcsb_binding_affinity annotations specifying measurement type, value, and units. Ligand SMILES were obtained from the RCSB chemical component REST endpoint and canonicalised with RDKit; entries with invalid or non-drug-like structures were discarded.

Rather than using PDB chain sequences directly — which may represent truncated constructs, fusion proteins, or alternative isoforms — each PDB entry was assigned the corresponding full-length training database sequence based on the matched UniProt accession. This choice ensures that ESM-2 embeddings at evaluation time are numerically identical to those seen during training, providing a fair measure of the trained model's generalisation rather than sensitivity to sequence perturbation. Binding affinity annotations (primarily Ki and IC50 values in nM) were converted to pIC50, and pairs with affinity at or below 10,000 nM were labelled active while pairs exceeding this threshold were labelled inactive. After deduplication on (canonical SMILES, protein sequence) and removal of entries with non-standard amino-acid sequences, the final benchmark comprised 1,115 unique compound–protein pairs from 1,112 PDB entries, covering 192 of the 425 training targets, with 954 active and 161 inactive pairs. Evaluation metrics for binary classification were AUC-ROC, AUPRC, enrichment factors at 1% and 5% (EF@1%, EF@5%), and BEDROC with α = 20. Regression performance was characterised by MAE, RMSE, and Spearman and Pearson correlations on the pIC50 values of active pairs. Confidence intervals for all metrics were computed by non-parametric bootstrap resampling (n = 2,000 replicates) with 95% coverage probability.

### 2.6 Chemical Similarity and Generalisation Analysis

To characterise the relationship between training-set proximity and test-set performance, we computed, for each PDBbind test compound, the maximum Tanimoto similarity to any compound in a random sample of 30,000 ChEMBL training actives using Morgan fingerprints (radius 2, 2,048 bits). The resulting nearest-neighbour Tanimoto value for each test compound was used to assign compounds to four quartiles by similarity, and per-quartile AUC-ROC was computed to assess whether model performance degrades for chemically novel test compounds.

### 2.7 Calibration Analysis

Model calibration was assessed by a reliability diagram (15 equal-width bins from 0 to 1) comparing mean predicted binding probability to observed fraction of actives within each bin, and by the expected calibration error (ECE), which measures the weighted mean absolute deviation between predicted probabilities and empirical frequencies.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Training Convergence

TargetTrace converged steadily over 18 epochs before early stopping was triggered. Training loss decreased monotonically from 0.134 at epoch 1 to 0.066 at epoch 18, reflecting continuous learning without plateau or instability. Validation AUC-ROC (protein-shuffle protocol) improved rapidly during the first four epochs, rising from 0.761 to approximately 0.767, and subsequently stabilised, reaching a best value of 0.771 at epoch 13 and plateauing thereafter. Validation AUPRC followed a similar trajectory, reaching 0.737 at convergence. The pIC50 MAE improved progressively from 0.620 to 0.611, indicating that the regression head continued to benefit from training even after the classification objective had largely stabilised.

### 3.2 Internal Validation Performance

Evaluated on the scaffold-split validation set under the protein-shuffle protocol, the final model achieves high discrimination performance. An AUC-ROC of 0.950 and AUPRC of 0.947 indicate that the model reliably assigns higher binding probability to correct molecule–protein pairs relative to randomly shuffled pairings. The BEDROC score of 0.989 and maximum enrichment factors (EF@1% = EF@5% = 2.0, corresponding to the theoretical maximum given the dataset composition) confirm that true binding pairs are systematically concentrated at the top of the ranked list. For pIC50 regression, the model achieves Spearman ρ = 0.667 and Pearson r = 0.644, indicating moderate but consistent ranking of compound potencies within and across targets.

### 3.3 External Evaluation on PDBbind

On the 1,115-pair PDBbind external benchmark, which tests molecule-level discrimination between co-crystallised active compounds and experimentally measured inactives against the same protein targets, the full TargetTrace model achieves strong and statistically well-supported performance across all evaluated metrics. The AUC-ROC of 0.854 (95% CI: 0.823–0.882) demonstrates that the model reliably separates actives from inactives under conditions that closely mirror prospective virtual screening. The AUPRC of 0.969 (0.957–0.978) reflects high precision at all recall levels despite the imbalanced class distribution (954 actives, 161 inactives). The near-perfect BEDROC of 0.994 (0.984–1.000) confirms that active compounds are heavily concentrated at the top of the model's ranked output, which is the operationally relevant property for prioritising candidates in drug discovery workflows. Regression performance on pIC50 yields a Spearman ρ of 0.628 (0.579–0.674) and Pearson r of 0.599 (0.549–0.649), showing that the continuous potency estimates are well-correlated with experimental values across the diverse 192-target PDBbind set. The narrow confidence intervals across all metrics confirm that these results are stable and not artefacts of the specific test-set composition.

### 3.4 Ablation Study

To isolate the contribution of each architectural and training component, four progressive model configurations were evaluated on the same PDBbind benchmark. The results are summarised in Table 4.

**Table 4. Ablation study: progressive configurations evaluated on PDBbind.**

| Configuration | Cross-Attention | Loss | Negatives | Val AUC | PDBbind AUC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1-Layer, BCE (shuffle-neg) | 1-layer | BCE | Shuffle only | 0.680 | 0.680 |
| +Focal loss, hard negatives | 2-layer | Focal (γ=2) | Shuffle + hard-protein | 0.777 | 0.538 |
| +2-layer attn, BCE | 2-layer | BCE | Shuffle only | 0.795 | 0.487 |
| Full model | 2-layer | BCE | Shuffle + real-molecule | 0.771 | **0.854** |

The simplest configuration — a single cross-attention layer with binary cross-entropy loss and protein-shuffle negatives — achieves PDBbind AUC = 0.680 and serves as the starting reference for the ablation. Replacing the BCE loss with focal loss (γ = 2) and augmenting the negative set with hard protein-space negatives — the ten most ESM-2-similar proteins per query — raises internal validation AUC to 0.777 but reduces PDBbind AUC to 0.538. Focal loss with γ = 2 progressively down-weights the gradient contribution of already-confident predictions; in combination with the model's high confidence on most positive pairs, this suppresses the training signal on the classification boundary and impairs generalisation. Hard protein-space negatives further bias the model toward distinguishing similar proteins rather than discriminating molecules of differing activity against a fixed target.

Removing focal loss and hard negatives while retaining the second cross-attention layer — with standard BCE and protein-shuffle negatives only — raises internal validation AUC to 0.795, the highest across all four configurations, while collapsing PDBbind AUC to 0.487, a performance indistinguishable from random. Inspection of the score distributions for this configuration reveals that both actives and inactives receive sigmoid scores concentrated near 1.0 (active mean 0.887, inactive mean 0.747, both medians near 0.99), rendering them indistinguishable by ranking. This directly confirms that the 2-layer cross-attention has learned, with high fidelity, to assign elevated binding probability to any (molecule, correct-protein) pair regardless of whether the molecule is genuinely active against that protein. The two tasks — protein-identity discrimination and molecule-activity discrimination — require qualitatively different negative evidence. Incorporating experimentally measured inactive pairs as explicit training negatives restores and dramatically improves PDBbind AUC to 0.854, a gain of 36.7 percentage points over the preceding configuration and 17.4 percentage points over the starting configuration.

### 3.5 Score Distributions and Model Calibration

The full model exhibits clear bimodal separation between active and inactive score distributions on PDBbind: actives concentrate near 1.0 (mean 0.81), while inactives show a prominent peak near 0.05 with a long tail toward higher probabilities (mean 0.34). This pattern contrasts sharply with the 2-layer shuffle-only configuration, in which both distributions are compressed near 1.0, making ranking-based discrimination impossible regardless of the chosen decision threshold. The calibration reliability diagram reveals, however, that the full model is substantially overconfident: the expected calibration error (ECE) is 0.178, and predicted probabilities in the mid-range (0.2–0.8) are assigned to relatively few test pairs. This bi-clustering is a consequence of the BCE training objective, which rewards high confidence on correctly labelled examples and does not penalise probability miscalibration explicitly. For ranking-based virtual screening applications, overconfidence does not impair performance, as AUC, AUPRC, and BEDROC are rank-based metrics unaffected by the absolute probability scale. However, calibration recalibration techniques such as Platt scaling or temperature scaling should be applied before using the model's raw outputs as probabilities for decision-making under uncertainty.

### 3.6 Per-Target and Per-Family Performance

Of the 192 targets in the PDBbind benchmark, 29 had sufficient representation (≥ 3 actives and ≥ 1 inactive) for per-target AUC computation. The mean per-target AUC across these 29 targets was 0.858 (SD 0.143), closely tracking the aggregate AUC of 0.854. Performance varied substantially across protein families. Isoprenoid biosynthesis enzymes, anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins, and HAT/bromodomain-containing proteins achieved mean AUCs of 1.000, 1.000, and 0.973 respectively. Receptor tyrosine kinases, aspartic proteases, and aldo-keto reductases all scored above 0.960. At the lower end, glycogen phosphorylases achieved 0.538, only marginally above random, and carbonic anhydrases achieved 0.748.

The underperformance on carbonic anhydrase II (CA-II) is particularly notable because this single target contributes 158 of the 1,115 pairs (14.2% of the benchmark), making it the dominant contributor to the aggregate AUC. CA-II binds a chemically diverse set of sulfonamides, coumarin-based inhibitors, and thiol ligands; its active site is solvent-exposed, small, and relatively rigid, placing a premium on precise pharmacophore geometry that a fingerprint-based model without 3D information cannot fully capture. The glycogen phosphorylase result may reflect the absence of allosteric pocket diversity in the training data, as most ChEMBL inhibitors target the active site, while PDBbind structures include indole-based allosteric binders at the purine nucleoside site.

### 3.7 Chemical Similarity and Generalisation to Novel Scaffolds

Of the 1,115 PDBbind test compounds, 24.7% (n = 275) have a maximum Tanimoto similarity below 0.4 to any compound in the ChEMBL training set, classifying them as genuinely novel scaffolds under standard medicinal chemistry definitions. Stratification by similarity quartile reveals that model performance degrades gracefully rather than catastrophically for novel compounds: the most-similar quartile (Q4, Tanimoto 0.73–1.00) achieves AUC = 0.929, while the most-novel quartile (Q1, Tanimoto 0.05–0.40) achieves AUC = 0.766 — still substantially above random. Intermediate quartiles achieve AUCs of 0.802 (Q2) and 0.860 (Q3), showing a monotonic relationship between training-set proximity and test performance. This pattern suggests that TargetTrace learns genuine structure-activity representations that extend to scaffolds not seen during training, rather than relying primarily on memorised chemical series. Nonetheless, the 16.3-point AUC gap between the most-novel and most-similar quartiles indicates that chemical novelty remains a meaningful performance determinant, and models intended for deployment on structurally diverse libraries should be tested accordingly.

### 3.8 Cross-Attention Weight Analysis

Inspection of the cross-attention weights over residue positions for representative drug-target pairs provides interpretable insight into the protein positions that the model attends to during scoring. For the carbonic anhydrase II / sulfonamide complex (PDB: 6SBH), Layer 1 attention is distributed across the sequence but shows elevated weights in the region surrounding residues 90–120, broadly consistent with the zinc-coordinating histidines H94, H96, and H119 that define the CA-II active site. Layer 2 attention further concentrates this signal and additionally highlights a secondary peak near residue 195–200, consistent with the gate residue T199 known to form a hydrogen bond with sulfonamide inhibitors. For the EP300/p300 HAT domain complex (PDB: 5KJ2), cross-attention weights in Layer 1 are distributed broadly across the long sequence (560 residues) but show elevated signal in the C-terminal HAT domain region where the acetyl-CoA cofactor and substrate lysine reside. These observations are qualitative rather than mechanistically validated — cross-attention weights are not directly interpretable as binding-site identifiers — but they suggest that the model has learned to preferentially weight regions of the protein sequence with functional relevance to ligand binding.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 The Critical Role of Molecule-Level Negative Training

The ablation results expose a fundamental tension in DTI model training that has received limited systematic attention in the literature. Protein-shuffle negatives and real molecule-level inactives address distinct discriminative sub-problems. Protein-shuffle negatives generate an easily separable signal: randomly associated (molecule, protein) pairs are almost always chemically incoherent, and a model can learn to reject them by encoding broad compatibility features such as binding-site chemical type or molecular size. This produces strong gradient signal and high validation AUC on the shuffle protocol, but it does not require the model to distinguish a highly potent inhibitor from a structurally similar but inactive congener presented to the same target. Real inactive pairs, by contrast, present the model with drug-like compounds — many of which are crystallographic ligands or close analogues — that nonetheless fail to reach the activity threshold. Learning to separate these from actives requires the development of genuine structure-activity reasoning: sensitivity to substituent positioning, pharmacophore complementarity, and binding-energy-relevant features. The 36.7-point AUC improvement on PDBbind resulting solely from the addition of measured inactives is the direct empirical consequence of providing this qualitatively different training signal.

### 4.2 Misalignment Between Internal and External Validation

The 2-layer + BCE configuration is instructive not only for what it achieves but for what it reveals about the risk of relying on a single internal metric for model selection. It achieves the highest internal validation AUC of any tested configuration (0.795) while simultaneously performing at chance level on the external benchmark (0.487). This discrepancy arises directly from the misalignment between the shuffle-negative training objective and the molecule-discrimination deployment objective. In practice, it means that a researcher using shuffle-negative AUC as the sole model selection criterion would preferentially retain the configuration with the worst real-world performance. Future work should incorporate a held-out molecule-discrimination validation set — comprising inactive pairs drawn from the training database — as a secondary model selection criterion aligned with the deployment objective. Such a criterion would directly penalise configurations that conflate protein-identity with activity discrimination.

### 4.3 Stacked Cross-Attention and Residue-Level Protein Representation

The two-layer stacked cross-attention mechanism with residual connection provides a principled framework for iterative refinement of the protein context conditioned on the molecular query. The first cross-attention layer produces a coarse summary of the protein's residue embedding space, weighted by global molecular features; the second layer refines this summary by re-querying residue positions conditioned on the updated protein representation, allowing attention weights to shift toward residues that are most relevant given the first-layer context. Analysis of the attention weights for well-characterised drug-target pairs (Section 3.8) shows that the model directs elevated attention toward functionally relevant regions — the zinc-coordinating histidines and gate residue in CA-II, and the HAT domain in EP300 — though this behaviour emerges from the training signal rather than from explicit binding-site supervision. The residual connection ensures that discriminative signals identified in the first layer are preserved even if the second layer learns primarily a correction. Caching the full per-residue ESM-2 tensor on GPU as a shared array allows O(1) protein lookup during training without repeated encoder forward passes, making the approach computationally feasible for large-scale training.

### 4.4 Generalisation and the Role of Chemical Diversity

The chemical similarity analysis (Section 3.7) establishes that TargetTrace generalises meaningfully beyond memorised training series. The 16.3-point AUC gap between the most-novel and most-similar test-set quartiles indicates a smooth, monotonic dependence on training-set proximity rather than a sharp drop-off, which is consistent with a model that has internalised chemical features of bioactivity rather than one that retrieves by nearest-neighbour lookup. This generalisation is likely driven by the combination of multi-radius fingerprints capturing complementary structural features at different scales, and the cross-attention mechanism providing protein-conditional weighting of molecular queries that is not available to a fixed-vector classifier. The fact that the most-novel quartile still achieves AUC = 0.766 — comparable to what a conventional fingerprint-based classifier achieves on average over the full benchmark — suggests that the model's learned representations carry substantial transferable signal. This result is practically important because prospective virtual screening campaigns most commonly target novel chemical series, precisely the regime where the model retains meaningful discriminative power.

### 4.5 Model Calibration and Practical Implications

The ECE of 0.178 indicates that the model is substantially overconfident: the output distribution is heavily bi-modal, with most predictions concentrated near 0 or 1, and mid-range probabilities are poorly calibrated. This overconfidence is a direct consequence of training with BCE loss, which drives the model to maximise log-likelihood by assigning extreme probabilities to correctly classified examples, without any term penalising miscalibration. For ranking-based applications such as virtual screening prioritisation, overconfidence does not impair performance — AUC, AUPRC, and BEDROC are rank-based metrics unaffected by the absolute scale of predicted probabilities. However, for probability-based decision criteria such as expected-value thresholding or Bayesian active learning acquisition functions, the raw sigmoid outputs should not be used directly. Post-hoc calibration via temperature scaling — which divides all logits by a single learned scalar before sigmoid — is a simple and effective remedy that preserves ranking performance while aligning the output scale with empirical frequencies. We recommend applying temperature scaling on a held-out calibration set before any probability-threshold-dependent use of the model.

### 4.6 pIC50 Prediction Quality

The external pIC50 Spearman correlation of 0.628 (95% CI: 0.579–0.674) is moderate but interpretable in the context of the evaluation task. The PDBbind benchmark spans 192 distinct protein targets representing diverse structural families, assay formats, and binding geometries, making target-agnostic pIC50 ranking inherently challenging: affinities measured in different assay formats against structurally unrelated targets are not directly comparable on a single scale. The observed MAE of 1.084 pIC50 units (95% CI: 1.015–1.152) corresponds to roughly one order of magnitude in Ki, consistent with the known difficulty of affinity prediction from sequence and 2D structure alone. Per-target performance is likely substantially higher than the aggregate metric suggests, but individual target sample sizes within the PDBbind benchmark are too small for reliable stratified reporting. The joint training objective provides useful regularisation — the regression head encourages the model to learn graded rather than binary binding signals — and the shared representation benefits from the larger training signal available through the classification task.

### 4.7 Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations of the present work merit consideration. The model does not use three-dimensional structural information at any stage; integrating predicted binding poses from docking or directly from AlphaFold2/ESMFold backbone predictions could substantially improve both classification accuracy and pIC50 precision, particularly for targets where the binding-site geometry is a primary selectivity determinant. The training data originate exclusively from ChEMBL and inherit its measurement biases: certain target classes — kinases, GPCRs, proteases — and their associated chemical series are heavily over-represented, and the model's performance on under-represented target families or on chemotypes distant from the training distribution remains uncharacterised. The global pIC50 normalisation conflates targets with intrinsically different potency ranges, potentially introducing systematic bias in regression estimates. The model's overconfidence (ECE = 0.178) is an important practical caveat for probability-based applications, and temperature scaling should be applied before using the model's raw outputs as calibrated probabilities. The per-target analysis identifies glycogen phosphorylases and carbonic anhydrases as performance outliers where target-specific fine-tuning or inclusion of allosteric binding-mode annotations may be needed.

Promising directions for future work include per-target pIC50 normalisation to correct for between-assay systematic offsets; integration of structure-based features from ESMFold or molecular docking to capture binding-site geometry; extension to kinase selectivity profiling using the 425-target coverage; a held-out molecule-discrimination validation set as a secondary early-stopping criterion; and prospective active learning with wet-laboratory confirmation to close the training–deployment loop.

### 4.8 Conclusions

TargetTrace demonstrates that the interaction between model architecture and negative-sampling strategy is a critical but frequently overlooked determinant of real-world DTI model performance. Architectural improvements in protein representation — specifically, the addition of a second cross-attention layer — produce gains in internal validation AUC that do not translate to external performance in the absence of molecule-level negative training. Conversely, the addition of experimentally measured inactive compound–target pairs provides a decisive improvement in external molecule-discrimination performance, raising PDBbind AUC from 0.487 to 0.854 (95% CI: 0.823–0.882). Chemical similarity analysis confirms that 24.7% of the test set consists of genuinely novel scaffolds, yet performance remains well above chance (AUC 0.766) for these compounds, demonstrating meaningful structural generalisation. Attention weight analysis provides preliminary evidence that the cross-attention layers assign elevated weight to functionally relevant protein regions without explicit binding-site supervision. The model is overconfident (ECE 0.178), and temperature scaling is recommended before using its outputs as calibrated probabilities. The primary practical implication of this work is that the source and biological meaning of negative training examples must be treated as a first-class design decision in DTI model development, not as an afterthought to architectural choices.
