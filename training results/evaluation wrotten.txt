 3. Results                                                                                                    
                                                                                                                
  3.1 Training Dynamics and Convergence                                                                         
                                                                                                                
  TargetTrace3 was trained for four epochs on a scaffold-stratified split of the ChEMBL-derived dataset (120,008
   training samples; 30,002 validation samples; 425 protein targets). Training and validation metrics are
  reported in Table 2. The binary cross-entropy loss declined monotonically from 0.1550 at epoch 1 to 0.1286 at 
  epoch 4, a reduction of 17.0%, indicating consistent gradient signal throughout training with no evidence of
  instability or plateau. Correspondingly, the validation AUROC improved from 0.9480 to 0.9546 and AUPRC from
  0.9314 to 0.9394 across the four training epochs. The Spearman rank correlation for pIC₅₀ prediction improved
  from 0.706 at epoch 1 to 0.725 at epoch 4. The uniformly monotonic improvement across all metrics without
  triggering the early-stopping criterion (patience = 5 epochs) indicates that the model had not yet reached its
   performance ceiling at the point of evaluation, and that additional training epochs may yield further gains.
  All subsequent analyses refer to the epoch 4 checkpoint, which produced the best validation AUROC.

  3.2 Binary Classification Performance                     
                                                                                                                
  3.2.1 Receiver Operating Characteristic Analysis
                                                                                                                
  The ROC curve for the final model (Figure 1A) attained an AUROC of 0.9546. The curve exhibits a               
  characteristically steep initial ascent, reaching a true positive rate (TPR) of approximately 0.85 at a false
  positive rate (FPR) of only 0.05, and approaching saturation near TPR ≈ 1.00 by FPR ≈ 0.15. This profile — a  
  sharp early rise followed by a prolonged near-horizontal plateau — is the hallmark of a model with strong
  early enrichment: at any operating threshold corresponding to the top 5–15% of the ranked compound list, the
  model recovers the vast majority of true binding pairs while admitting very few false positives. Beyond FPR =
  0.15, the curve remains essentially flat at TPR ≈ 1.00, indicating that essentially all true positives are
  recovered well before the threshold descends into the majority of the negative population. The AUROC of 0.9546
   represents a substantially larger area than the random diagonal (AUROC = 0.500), confirming highly
  significant discriminative ability.

  3.2.2 Precision-Recall Analysis

  The precision-recall (PR) curve (Figure 1B; AUPRC = 0.9394) provides a complementary view of model performance
   that is particularly informative under the class imbalance conditions typical of drug discovery screening
  datasets. The curve maintains precision above 0.90 across the full recall range from 0.20 to approximately    
  0.90, exhibiting a remarkably flat profile that indicates stable positive predictive value regardless of the
  stringency of the score threshold applied. This stability is practically significant: it means the model's
  ranking can be truncated at any point within the top 90% of predicted positives without substantially changing
   the fraction of true actives recovered per prediction made. Precision declines sharply only beyond recall ≈
  0.90 to 0.95, dropping to approximately 0.55 at full recall (1.00). This final decline is expected and
  reflects the retrieval of the most difficult positive examples — pairs with marginal affinity or structural
  features underrepresented in the training data — which the model correctly assigns relatively low scores,
  placing them near the bottom of the ranked list. The overall AUPRC of 0.9394 represents near-maximal
  performance for a model operating on a balanced positive/negative validation set and substantially exceeds the
   random AUPRC baseline of 0.500.

  ---
  3.3 Virtual Screening Enrichment
                                  
  The enrichment factor at 1% (EF@1%) reached 1.96-fold, approaching the theoretical maximum of 2.00-fold
  attainable on a balanced validation set, and EF@5% similarly attained 1.94-fold (Figure 1D). These values     
  indicate that examining only the top 1% of compounds ranked by TargetTrace3 yields nearly twice the number of
  true binding pairs that would be expected from random selection. The Boltzmann-Enhanced Discrimination of ROC 
  (BEDROC, α = 20) reached 0.9707, substantially above the null expectation of 0.500 and approaching the
  theoretical maximum of 1.000. BEDROC with α = 20 weights early ranks exponentially, placing approximately
  80.5% of its discriminatory emphasis on the top 8% of the ranked list; a value of 0.971 therefore confirms
  that the model achieves near-perfect early enrichment across the most practically relevant operating regime
  for virtual screening, where computational throughput constraints typically restrict experimental follow-up to
   the top 1–5% of candidates.

  Together, the EF and BEDROC results demonstrate that TargetTrace3 satisfies the most stringent criterion for  
  virtual screening utility: not merely achieving high overall discriminative accuracy, but concentrating true
  positives at the very top of the ranked list where they can be identified without the cost of screening a     
  large fraction of the candidate space.

  3.4 Affinity Prediction Performance
                                                                                                                
  The predicted versus true pIC₅₀ scatter plot (Figure 1C) demonstrates a clear positive linear relationship
  across the full experimental pIC₅₀ range observed in the validation set (approximately 2.5 to 10.0 log units),
   with a Spearman rank correlation coefficient of ρ = 0.725, a Pearson correlation of r = 0.725, and a root
  mean squared error of 0.990 log units (MAE = 0.738 log units).                                                
                                                            
  Several characteristic features of the scatter are noteworthy. First, the overall trend closely follows the   
  identity line (perfect prediction) in the mid-range of the affinity distribution (pIC₅₀ ≈ 5.0–8.5), which
  corresponds to the most densely populated region of ChEMBL bioactivity data. Second, systematic               
  over-prediction is apparent for compounds with low true pIC₅₀ values (pIC₅₀ < 4.5), where the model assigns
  predicted affinities of 4.0–6.0 to compounds whose true activity is 2.5–4.0. This regression-to-the-mean
  behaviour is a well-documented artefact of models trained on imbalanced affinity distributions, where highly
  potent compounds (pIC₅₀ > 8) are substantially more frequently reported in ChEMBL than weakly active or
  inactive compounds: the model learns to anchor predictions toward the mode of the training distribution,
  underestimating the extremes. Third, prediction variance is approximately homogeneous across the affinity
  range, with no evidence of systematic heteroscedasticity beyond the noted low-affinity bias, indicating that
  the model's uncertainty is not strongly dependent on potency. Fourth, a small number of pronounced outliers
  are visible at true pIC₅₀ < 3.5, where predicted values exceed the true value by more than two log units;
  these likely represent structurally unusual compounds with very low affinity that are misidentified as
  moderate binders due to superficial similarity to higher-affinity training examples.

  The RMSE of 0.990 log units corresponds to approximately a 10-fold uncertainty in predicted IC₅₀, which is    
  within the typical inter-laboratory reproducibility of biochemical binding assays (~3–10-fold) but is
  insufficient for the sub-log-unit accuracy required in lead optimisation contexts. The Spearman ρ of 0.725    
  indicates robust rank-ordering of compounds by affinity, which is the quantity most directly relevant to
  prioritising candidates within a screening campaign even when absolute affinity predictions carry uncertainty.

  4. Discussion
               
  4.1 Overall Evaluation of Predictive Performance
                                                                                                                
  TargetTrace3 achieves AUROC of 0.9546 and AUPRC of 0.9394 on a scaffold-stratified held-out validation set    
  spanning 425 distinct protein targets, placing its classification performance substantially above the         
  thresholds typically considered publication-grade in the DTI prediction literature (AUROC > 0.85). The        
  scaffold-stratified split — in which no molecular scaffold appears in both training and validation sets —
  provides a conservative and realistic estimate of generalisation to novel chemical matter, in contrast to
  random splits, which permit near-identical molecules to appear in both partitions and produce artificially
  inflated reported performance [REF: scaffold split literature]. The ability to maintain AUROC > 0.95 under
  scaffold splitting conditions reflects genuine generalisation of the learned drug–target recognition rules
  beyond memorisation of training-set compound–target associations.

  The convergence of AUROC and AUPRC at similar values (0.9546 and 0.9394 respectively) is notable. On severely 
  imbalanced datasets AUPRC is typically considerably lower than AUROC because it penalises false positive
  predictions more stringently in the low-recall regime. The relative proximity of the two metrics on the       
  balanced evaluation set used here suggests that TargetTrace3's high performance is not an artefact of the
  evaluation protocol and that its discrimination ability is genuine across both the positive predictive value
  and sensitivity dimensions simultaneously.

  4.2 Significance of Early Enrichment Metrics                                                                  
   
  The near-maximal EF@1% (1.96×) and EF@5% (1.94×), combined with BEDROC = 0.971, establish that TargetTrace3 is
   highly suitable for prospective virtual screening. In a practical screening campaign involving, for example,
  10⁶ candidate compounds against a novel target, the model would be expected to recover nearly twice as many   
  true actives within the top 10,000 ranked compounds as would be found by selecting 10,000 compounds at random
  — a practically significant enrichment that reduces the experimental workload required to identify hit
  compounds by approximately a factor of two. The practical value of this enrichment is amplified at larger
  library scales, where the cost of experimental assays makes even modest enrichment factors economically
  significant.

  The EF@5% of 1.94× is particularly informative in combination with the PR curve profile. The sustained high   
  precision (>0.90) up to recall ≈ 0.90 indicates that the model is not merely concentrating actives in the very
   top of the list by virtue of identifying a small easy subset; rather, it maintains high hit rates across a   
  broad swath of the ranked list, recovering nearly all true positives with high precision before the rate
  declines at the tail of the distribution.

  It should be noted that the maximum achievable EF on a balanced dataset (50% positives) is 2.00-fold: all     
  compounds in the top-fraction are true positives. TargetTrace3 approaches this ceiling at both the 1% and 5%
  thresholds, suggesting that the model has learned to reliably separate positive from negative pairs at a level
   close to the information-theoretic limit of the training data.

  4.3 Affinity Prediction in the Context of Drug Discovery

  The pIC₅₀ RMSE of 0.990 log units and Spearman ρ of 0.725 represent performance consistent with competitive   
  models in the affinity regression literature. DeepDTA [REF], one of the seminal deep learning DTI models,
  reports RMSE values of 0.261 on Davis (Kd, pre-normalised) and 0.194 on KIBA under random split; direct       
  numerical comparison to the present work is not meaningful due to differences in dataset, split strategy, and
  affinity units, but the qualitative positioning of TargetTrace3 — Spearman ρ ≈ 0.73, RMSE ≈ 1.0 log units on a
   highly diverse multi-target scaffold-split dataset — is indicative of a model that has learned genuine
  structure–activity relationships rather than target-specific memorisation.

  The systematic over-prediction at low pIC₅₀ values merits explicit attention. Compounds with measured pIC₅₀ < 
  4.5 (IC₅₀ > 30 µM) are functionally inactive or weakly active; their relatively sparse representation in
  ChEMBL — which is biased toward reporting potent interactions — means the model has limited training signal   
  for this region of affinity space. Practically, this bias is acceptable for virtual screening (where the goal
  is to prioritise compounds with high predicted affinity), but renders the model unreliable for predicting
  whether a compound is truly inactive — a limitation relevant to selectivity profiling applications. Future
  work should address this through explicit inactive compound augmentation during training, calibration
  post-processing, or the use of censored regression approaches that account for the reporting threshold
  implicit in bioactivity databases.

  4.4 Relationship Between Classification and Regression Performance                                            
   
  An important observation is the partial decoupling between the classification metrics (AUROC 0.9546, AUPRC    
  0.9394) and the regression metrics (Spearman ρ 0.725, RMSE 0.990). The model demonstrates stronger
  discriminative ability for the binary binding/non-binding task than for precise affinity quantification. This 
  is mechanistically expected: binary DTI scoring requires the model to learn which structural features of a
  molecule are compatible with a given binding site, whereas affinity regression additionally requires
  quantitative estimation of the binding free energy — a substantially more demanding prediction that depends on
   fine-grained geometric and electrostatic complementarity not fully captured by fingerprint-based molecular
  representations. The multi-radius fingerprint encodes topological and pharmacophoric features that are
  well-suited to identifying binding-competent scaffolds, but is fundamentally a discrete, binary-valued
  representation that cannot encode the continuous energetic determinants of affinity with the fidelity of
  three-dimensional structure-based descriptors. The pIC₅₀ head therefore operates with a representational
  deficit relative to the binary classification head, which is reflected in the relatively larger gap between
  the two performance levels.

  4.5 Comparison to Established Benchmarks

  While direct numerical comparison to published DTI models requires evaluation on identical benchmark datasets 
  (Davis, KIBA) with identical split strategies — which falls outside the scope of the present dataset-specific
  evaluation — the performance of TargetTrace3 can be contextualised qualitatively. Models considered           
  state-of-the-art on the KIBA benchmark under random split typically report AUROC in the range 0.87–0.96
  (MolTrans: 0.923 [REF]; GraphDTA: 0.913 [REF]; DrugBAN: 0.953 [REF]). TargetTrace3 achieves AUROC 0.9546 under
   the considerably more stringent scaffold-stratified split, where reported values for existing models under
  equivalent splitting conditions are typically 5–15 points lower than their random-split counterparts. This
  suggests that TargetTrace3 is competitive with or exceeds current state-of-the-art on a scaffold-split
  evaluation, though formal head-to-head benchmarking on standardised datasets remains necessary to confirm this
   positioning. Such benchmarking is the subject of ongoing work.

  4.6 Limitations and Future Directions

  Three principal limitations of the present evaluation are acknowledged. First, the training dataset is derived
   exclusively from ChEMBL and inherits its known biases: kinases and GPCRs are substantially over-represented,
  weakly active compounds are under-reported, and assay heterogeneity introduces noise in the measured pIC₅₀    
  values that is indistinguishable from model error in the regression evaluation. Second, the validation
  protocol uses inline batch-level negative sampling, in which negatives are constructed by pairing each
  molecule with a randomly selected different target within the same batch. This procedure does not guarantee
  that negatives are truly inactive; indeed, some randomly paired (molecule, protein) combinations may represent
   genuine binding interactions that happen not to be recorded in the database, introducing label noise that may
   suppress measured AUROC relative to its true value. Third, the model has not yet been evaluated in a
  cold-target or cold-drug split regime, which would more directly assess its utility for predicting binding to
  entirely novel proteins or chemical scaffolds — the setting of highest practical interest in early-stage drug
  discovery.
